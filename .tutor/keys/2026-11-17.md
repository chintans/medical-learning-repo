# Answer key: Session 2 (2026-11-17)

## Warm-up
- **W1 B** -otomy = cut into. -ostomy = make a mouth (stoma), an opening kept open to the surface. In everyday clinical speech the two are sometimes used loosely, but the strict meanings differ. A would be a tracheal resection, C is bronchoscopy/tracheoscopy.
- **W2 C** -scopy = look inside with an instrument. Colectomy removes colon, colostomy brings colon out to the skin, colotomy is an incision into the colon. (A biopsy or polyp removal can happen during a colonoscopy, but the word itself only means looking.)

## Quiz
- **Q1 A** pneumo- here = air, -thorax = chest. Air in the pleural space. B is pneumonia, C is pleural effusion (or hydrothorax / haemothorax if blood), D is not a standard X-ray term like this. Tests the pneum- = air/lung trap.
- **Q2 B** haemat- (blood) + -oma (mass/collection) = a localised collection of blood outside vessels, common after surgery. A confuses -oma with cancer and haem- with haematological malignancy. C is a lipoma. D would be anaemia or "drop in Hb".
- **Q3 B** -algia = pain, a symptom. Osteoporosis is a disease (low bone density), carcinoma is a malignant tumour (finding/diagnosis), pneumonia is a disease (lung infection/inflammation).
- **Q4 B** cutaneous (Latin *cutis*, skin) is an anatomical adjective; dermat- (Greek) + -itis names inflammation. C is wrong: beneath the skin is *sub*cutaneous. D is wrong: -itis = inflammation, not infection; contact dermatitis is usually an irritant or allergic reaction, not an infection.
- **Q5 B** lip- = fat, -oma = mass. Lipoma is a benign tumour of fat cells. A is the main trap (melanoma and lymphoma are malignant, lipoma and haematoma are not). C would be panniculitis/"lipitis"-type words. D describes atheroma / fatty plaque (not taught).

## Q6 model answer (grade for the idea)
Medical words come from two language layers: Greek, used mostly for diseases, specialties and operations, and Latin, which became the language of anatomy. So one organ can be described by a Latin adjective (where it is) and named in disease words by its Greek root. Knowing both lets you link "renal" in a report to "nephropathy" in a paper and realise they are about the same organ. Valid pairs not in Q1–Q5: renal/nephr-, mammary/mast-, uterine/hyster-, oral/stomat-, osseous/oste-, hepatic/hepat- (both Greek, acceptable if he notes it), cardiac/cardi- (Greek-derived both), pulmonary/pneumon- is acceptable only if he chose it consciously—it was in Q1's topic, so half credit.
**Full credit:** gives the reason (two languages, different jobs), the practical benefit, and a valid new pair. **Half credit:** pair given but no reasoning, or reasoning without a correct pair.
