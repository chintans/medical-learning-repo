## Part B — Lesson (10 min)

### The big idea
Each body system has a small set of roots. Learn the root once and you can read the specialty name, the disease words and the procedure words for that whole system. The catch is that many organs have **two** roots, one Greek and one Latin. Doctors switch between them freely, so "renal" and "nephro-" are the same kidney.

### Mechanism: why two names for one organ?
Medical language grew in two layers. Greek physicians (Hippocrates, Galen) named most **diseases and their study**, and Latin became the language of **anatomy** during the European Renaissance. So a rough rule of thumb (a tendency, not a law):

- **Latin root → describes where something is** (an adjective of anatomy): *renal artery, pulmonary embolism, cutaneous rash*
- **Greek root → names a disease, a specialty or an operation**: *nephritis, pneumonia, dermatology, mastectomy*

```
          ONE ORGAN, TWO ROOTS
   Latin (anatomy, "where")   Greek (disease / specialty / surgery)
   ------------------------   -------------------------------------
   renal          (kidney)    nephritis, nephrologist, nephrectomy
   pulmonary      (lung)      pneumonia, pneumonectomy
   cutaneous      (skin)      dermatitis, dermatologist
   osseous        (bone)      osteoporosis, osteomyelitis
   mammary        (breast)    mastectomy, mastitis
   uterine        (uterus)    hysterectomy
   oral           (mouth)     stomatitis
```

Some organs use the Greek root for both jobs (*gastric* and *gastritis*, *hepatic* and *hepatitis*), so don't expect the rule to hold every time. What matters is recognising that both columns point to the same organ.

One more trap: **pneum-** means both *lung* (pneumonia) and *air* (pneumothorax = air in the chest cavity, around the lung, which can make the lung collapse). *-thorax* = chest.

**Two suffixes worth slowing down on:**

- **-oma** tells you there is a **lump or collection**, not whether it is dangerous. A *lipoma* (lip- = fat) is a benign fatty lump. A *carcinoma* (carcin- = cancer) is malignant. A *haematoma* is not a tumour at all: it is a pocket of blood that has leaked out of vessels, like a deep bruise. And a few -omas are malignant even though nothing else in the word warns you (*melanoma*, *lymphoma*). So **-oma never answers "is it cancer?" by itself.**
- **-algia** names a **symptom** (pain), not a cause. "Arthralgia" means the joints hurt. It does not say why. Arthr*itis* says the joint is inflamed, which is a finding.

### In the doctor's office
- In India you will often see **"fever with arthralgia and myalgia"** on a case sheet during the monsoon. That is a description of symptoms (joint and muscle pain), and the doctor then orders tests to find the cause (dengue, chikungunya and others are on the list).
- A scan report saying **"a 2 cm hypoechoic lesion"** or "-oma" type words is describing a lump. The right questions: *"Is this benign or malignant, or do we not know yet? What test would tell us?"* (often a biopsy, which is pathology, the para-clinical layer from Session 1).
- A **"haematoma"** after a fall, injection or surgery usually means trapped blood. Ask: *"Is it expected to absorb on its own, and what would make you drain it?"*
- **Specialist names decode the same way**: gastroenterologist (stomach + intestines), pulmonologist (lungs), neurologist (nerves and brain), haematologist (blood), dermatologist (skin), orthopaedician (bones and joints; *ortho-* = straight).

### In the journal
Paper titles mix both root families in one line: *"Pulmonary outcomes after community-acquired pneumonia"*, *"Incidence of osteoporotic fractures"*, *"Peripheral neuropathy as an adverse event"*. Pain words like *arthralgia* and *myalgia* turn up in the **adverse-events table** of drug trials. When you read one, remember it lists what patients *reported*, not proven causes.
