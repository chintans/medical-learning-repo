## Part B — Lesson (8 min, shorter than usual)

### The big idea
Knowing roots one at a time is step one. The real skill is reading a **whole line** of a medical note and knowing what kind of information each piece is. Every line of a case sheet is some mix of four things: what the patient **feels** (symptoms), what the doctor or a test **found** (findings), what the doctor **concluded** (diagnosis), and what is being **done** (action).

### Mechanism: a 3-step method for any note
1. **Expand the shorthand** (k/c/o, c/o, s/p, O/E, BD, SOS…).
2. **Split each medical word from the end** (suffix first, then root, then prefix), as in Session 1.
3. **Sort each piece into a bucket:** symptom → finding → diagnosis → action.

```
   SYMPTOM          FINDING                 DIAGNOSIS          ACTION
   (patient says)   (exam / test shows)     (doctor concludes) (what's done)
   ------------     -------------------     ----------------   -------------
   -algia, dys-     -megaly, tachy-,        -itis, -osis,      -ectomy, -scopy,
   "c/o ..."        "O/E ...", report lines  "Dx: ..."          "Rx: ...", "Advised ..."
```

Step 3 is what makes you sound like someone who understands medicine. It also shows you **what's still unexplained**, which is where your best questions come from.

### Worked example (a made-up summary, written for this lesson)

```
58 M. k/c/o T2DM, HTN × 10 yrs.
c/o fever with dysuria × 3 days; one episode of haematuria.
O/E: febrile, tachycardia. No dyspnoea.
USG abdomen: mild hepatomegaly. Both kidneys normal in size.
Dx: Acute cystitis.
Rx: Tab. [antibiotic] BD × 5 days. Tab. paracetamol SOS for fever.
Advice: Review with urine culture report.
```

Decoded:

| Line | Plain English | Bucket |
|---|---|---|
| k/c/o T2DM, HTN × 10 yrs | Known case of type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure for 10 years | Background (past diagnoses) |
| c/o fever with dysuria; haematuria | Complains of fever and painful urination; once noticed blood in the urine | Symptoms |
| O/E: febrile, tachycardia; no dyspnoea | On examination: has a fever, fast heart rate; no difficulty breathing | Findings (a "no" is a finding too) |
| USG: mild hepatomegaly; kidneys normal | Ultrasound: liver slightly enlarged; kidneys look normal | Findings (test) |
| Dx: acute cystitis | Conclusion: sudden-onset inflammation of the urinary bladder (usually infection) | Diagnosis |
| Rx / Advice | Antibiotic twice daily for 5 days; paracetamol only if fever; come back with the urine culture | Action |

**What sorting reveals:** the diagnosis (cystitis) explains the fever, dysuria and haematuria. It does **not** explain the enlarged liver. That's a loose end. The expert question is: *"The scan says the liver is slightly enlarged. Is that related to this infection, or something separate that needs its own follow-up?"*

### Terminology box (added to `glossary.md`)

| Part | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **dys-** | difficult, painful or abnormal | **dys**uria (painful urination), **dys**pnoea, **dys**pepsia (indigestion) |
| **a- / an-** | without, absence of | **an**aemia (literally "without blood": too little haemoglobin), **a**pnoea |
| **-uria** | condition of the urine | haemat**uria** (blood in urine), dys**uria**, an**uria** (no urine) |
| **-pnoea / -pnea** | breathing | dys**pnoea** (breathlessness), a**pnoea** (breathing stops), tachy**pnoea** (fast breathing) |
| **-rrhoea / -rrhea** | flow, discharge | dia**rrhoea** (literally "flowing through"), rhino**rrhoea** (runny nose) |
| **O/E** | on examination | "O/E: afebrile, BP stable" |

### In the doctor's office
- When you read a summary, look for the **"no ..." lines** (no dyspnoea, no haematuria). Doctors write these on purpose: they show the dangerous possibilities they checked for and ruled out. They're as informative as the positives.
- If a finding in the report isn't explained by the diagnosis, ask about it directly. Doctors usually welcome *"Is X related, or separate?"*. It shows you've read the report properly.
- Indian prescriptions often show the **brand name** with the **generic** below it, or only the brand. Asking "what's the generic name of this?" helps you look it up and compare prices. (We'll cover prescriptions properly in Module 2.4.)

### In the journal
The **Methods** section of a trial lists **inclusion and exclusion criteria** in this same vocabulary: e.g. *"adults with T2DM and HTN; excluded: haematuria, prior nephrectomy"*. Decoding these lines tells you **who the result applies to**. If you, or the person you're reading for, would have been excluded, the result may not fit them.
